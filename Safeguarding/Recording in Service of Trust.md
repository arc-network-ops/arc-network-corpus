# Recording in Service of Trust

*A sibling to* Protection in Service of Openness. *The ARC's principles, taxonomy, and norms for recording and the handling of voice. May 2026 — provisional, sourced by Daniel Thorson.*

> **Note.** This is an initial way of looking, calibrated to the present phase. It will be refined as the network matures.

---

## Orientation

This document sits alongside *Protection in Service of Openness*. That document concerns what happens to artifacts that already exist within the ARC. This one is upstream: it concerns when we record, what we record, who holds the recording, and what the network does with the record over time.

The reframe at the center is this. In most organizations, recording is done *to* members for the institution's benefit, and privacy is the protective shield against it. In the ARC, recording is meant to be done *for* the network, in service of a purpose its members share, with consent from all involved, named openly. The hope is that as trust grows, members will be glad to contribute their voice because they are participating in something whose purpose they share.

Two things are true about that hope. The first: even where the orientation is genuine, recording changes what is possible in a field — the body registers it, and what one can be vulnerable about narrows. The second: how recording lands varies meaningfully between people. For some, it deepens the sense of offering; for others, it activates internal monitoring. Both responses are real. The arrangements below are calibrated to honor that variance, and to the present phase, when trust is still being built.

---

## Principles

**Recording, done in relationship, builds trust rather than undermining it.** The default disposition is transparency about what is recorded, by whom, for what, and where it goes. This is the reverse of most organizational practice.

**Authority is structurally exposed, and the record protects both sides.** Those who hold roles with concentrated influence in the ARC accept structural recording as part of the role. The cost of holding authority in this network is being on the record. The more you can affect the field, the more the field can see what you do.

The record's protective function runs in both directions. It supports the authority by holding a clear account when memory drifts; it supports the member by holding a clear account when harm is contested. The reference is the police body camera as it works at its best — a record that the more-powerful party cannot selectively edit, and that gives the less-powerful party something to point to when their account is doubted. The record does not produce accountability on its own. It makes accountability more possible.

**Vulnerability is structurally protected.** The deeper the practice, the less it is archived. Spaces of greatest intimacy — dyadic practice, depth work — default to no recording.

**Containers carry agreement fields.** Each kind of container has its own recording posture, communicated on entry and restated in canonical openings. Within any shared container, the lower-comfort threshold sets the default; per-individual sensitivities are honored.

**The record is the recording and its imagined future.** What the body responds to in a recorded space is not only the recording but the body's sense of who will hear it, in what spirit, with what consequence. A recorded space whose imagined future is unclear, indefinite, or threatening will narrow the field even where the actual stewardship is careful. The recording arrangements below are designed so that the imagined future of the record is legible — what is kept, by whom, for how long, accessible to whom — because that legibility is part of what makes the record bearable.

**Records are held by the network, not by individuals — and the network's holding is built.** The Source records his side of all ARC conversations — group containers, 1:1s, dyads, governance — and others in positions of authority in the ARC will likely do the same as the network matures. These recordings are held on the network's behalf and committed to a dedicated stewardship role — the *Steward of Information Integrity* — as that role is filled. They are not anyone's personal archive.

Until that role is filled, the Source is the provisional holder. This is named honestly: a temporary double-holding by the person whom the record is also meant to expose. The honesty is the point. The arrangement is not the destination; the trajectory toward the destination is part of what this principle commits to.

The reasons for the structure are two. First, accountability: in contemplative communities, those who hold concentrated influence are commonly subject to projection and memory drift around what they actually said. A clear record makes drift harder and gives the network something to look at if and when concerns arise. Second, anti-cult design: power that holds its own record is harder to challenge. By committing these recordings to the network rather than to the individuals who hold authority — and by moving the actual holding outward as soon as the stewardship role is filled — the structure diffuses authority outward. The recordings exist not so that any individual has the receipts, but so that the network has them.

## Per-individual rights in recorded spaces

Within any recorded space, members hold the following affordances. They are part of what makes the recording bearable.

**The right to ask for edit or redaction.** Any member may request that specific material be removed from a recording or its transcript — a moment of acute personal disclosure, an identifying detail, an exchange that was made in a higher register of trust than the participant later wishes to extend to the record. The request is honored as a working default.

**The right to ask off-record.** In any recorded space, a member may name that something they are about to say is offered off-record, and the recording is paused or the material is excised in stewardship. This is not invoked casually; the field of recorded spaces depends on most of what happens in them being on the record. But it exists.

**The right to know what the record holds and where it goes.** A member may ask what recordings exist of them, where those recordings are held, who has access, and how long they will be retained. The honest answer — including "this hasn't been figured out yet" where that is true — is the response.

These rights are practical expressions of the principles above. They depend on the stewardship infrastructure functioning, which is itself a developing capacity. Where the infrastructure cannot yet fulfill a request, that is named openly.

## Taxonomy of spaces

What follows is offered for orientation — a first sketch of the kinds of containers in the network and how we mean to hold them at this stage. Specifics will evolve as the work continues, as new spaces emerge, and as the norms around recording, consent, and disclosure keep developing — both within the ARC and in the wider world.

Each entry below will, in time, specify three things: the retention window for material recorded in that space, who has access to it, and the per-individual affordances available there. These specifics are being worked through in conversation with members whose practice is most affected by them; the current draft holds the shape of the taxonomy rather than its final content.

**Foundations Training** (cohort one ran as the Unfolding Circle). Recorded; A/V shared back to the cohort for review; anonymized transcripts contribute to the training's context pool. The full agreement is in the training's Agreement Field.

**Open drop-in practice events** (public-facing, as offered). Recorded. Because the group changes week to week, the A/V recording is *opt-in per session* — those who want the recording afterward can ask for it. Anonymized transcripts contribute to a pool used for the development of artifacts, teaching, and curriculum.

**ARC group calls** (the weekly Gathering, and future regular member containers). Recorded; member-internal A/V shared back to members; anonymized transcripts contribute to the ARC context pool. The lower-comfort threshold and per-individual sensitivity rules apply.

**Dyadic practice.** No default recording. Both parties may consent to record for their own purposes; both must consent for any contribution beyond the dyad. The Weekly Weave, which routes members into these encounters, does not observe, record, or transcribe them — its specification names this among its refusals.

**Depth and special practice** — workshops, retreat-style work, specific teachings of a particularly tender nature. No recording, including no personal side-recording. These spaces are kept uncomplicated by being archived; that uncomplication is part of what makes the depth possible.

**Source 1:1s and dyadic work with the Source.** Recorded by the Source on his own side, as named in the principles above. This is disclosed in the canonical opening for the container.

*A note on AI tools used by members.* You're welcome to use AI tools to reflect on your own experience, including by uploading personal recordings. Norms in this territory are still developing, inside the ARC and in the wider world. The practice we ask for within the network: don't upload identifying information about others who haven't consented. If you want to bring a transcript of a shared container into an AI system, anonymize it first — remove names and identifying details before the upload.

## What this policy cannot do

Some of what a recording policy is normally asked to do, the ARC's policy cannot deliver, and naming this is part of the policy's honesty.

**External accountability does not yet exist for this network.** Regulated professions that record-and-retain under accountability frames — therapy, financial services, policing — operate under external regulators whose authority is not delegated by the recorded organization. The ARC has no analogue. The accountability layer described here is internal; the structural commitment to build toward external accountability over time is real but unfulfilled in the current phase. A member with a concern that internal structures cannot meet may need to take it outside the network. The right to do so is recognized.

**The record cannot enforce the sincerity of the recorded.** A person whose orientation has drifted toward instrumentalization can speak fluently in the recorded space, and the recording will not detect what is missing. The practice's sensitivity layer is what catches this — recorded material is supporting evidence, not the primary instrument of integrity. *(See: Safeguarding the Culture, Layer IV.)*

**Retention itself creates exposure.** Material kept can be subpoenaed, compromised, or leaked. The arrangements minimize what is retained for what is genuinely needed, but the residual risk is real and is borne by everyone whose material is in the record.

## What is still being clarified

As the network matures, additional structures will take shape — a dedicated stewardship role for information integrity (currently held by the Source as a temporary double-holding), norms for practice holders, distinct context pools for offerings beyond Foundations and the ARC core, and clearer protocols for situations not yet encountered. The principles above are intended to hold steady; the specifics will be filled in as the network grows into them.
